Kurikaeshi for your class

For whoever teaches the course. Written to be read on a phone, in the order you would do it. Matches Kurikaeshi 0.11.13, October 2026.

What it is

A flashcard app that decides when to show each word again: sooner while a student is forgetting it, later once it sticks. Five to fifteen minutes a day, on the student's own phone or laptop, and it keeps working offline.

It is free. Course decks come with a free account: a student signs in once, adds the deck with one tap, and says which lesson the class is on. The account also carries their progress to a second phone or a laptop, and both you and your students need one to use a class.

Kurikaeshi is an independent app, not run by your school. Students choose to use it, and nothing in it is a grade.

Three ways to give a class its cards

1. A course deck, by its link

These decks are already in the app. Each link opens the app on that one deck. A student signs in, the deck is added, and the app asks Which lesson is your class on? New cards then come from that lesson and the ones before it, a few a day. A student can change the lesson any time, or tap Choose lessons one by one.

2. A deck of your own

Send me your vocabulary as a spreadsheet headed word, reading, meaning, lesson, and I add it to the app as a course deck with its own link like the ones above.

You can also write the cards yourself. Tap Write my own cards, add your words, then open the deck and tap Share deck. Sharing needs you to be signed in. The link it gives you opens the deck for any student who signs in. Share again after a change, and the same link opens the new version. A deck you wrote yourself shares by link only; it cannot be published to a class.

3. A class

A class adds a roster and a live view of how students are doing.

  1. Sign in, then tap You, then Classes, then Create a class. Give it a title as students will see it, your name (asked only the first time), and the last day of the term.
  2. You get a six-character code. Hand it out, or tap Share class link for a link that carries it. New code retires the old code; students already on the roster stay.
  3. A student opens the link, or taps You, then Classes, then Join with a code. Before joining, they see the class title, your name, the end date, and one screen saying exactly what is shared, what is not, who sees it, and until when. They tick a box to agree.
  4. Each request lands on your roster. Tap Approve or Decline. Remove takes an approved student off.
  5. To publish a deck, add it on your own phone first (Decks, Add a deck). Then open the class, pick the deck, and tap Publish deck. Approved students install it from the class's page. A course deck or a deck file can be published this way; cards you wrote yourself cannot.

What you see once students are studying

Open the class and tap Open the class view. It reads the reviews approved students chose to share, of the decks you published to that class, and answers four questions. Only students who signed in and joined appear in it.

  1. Which cards the class is failing, ranked by how often a card was wrong on first sight, then how often it keeps being forgotten. This is the list to bring to class.
  2. Whether the class is on pace, per lesson: how many students have seen none, some, most or all of it. A spread, not an average, because an average hides the half of the room that has not started.
  3. Who has stopped, by reviews in the last seven days and the date each student was last active. Dates only.
  4. Whether your correction landed: which version of the deck each student is on.

Copy report copies the answers as text, and a copy you saved stays yours even after a student leaves. Nothing else is kept on your device after you leave the view.

The view says on its own face what it is: reported by each student's own device, from a record they chose to share. Treat it as a submitted worksheet: evidence of what was done, not proof. Nothing here is a grade, and nothing in the app can tell you whether a student was truthful.

The class closes by itself after its last day, and that date cannot be changed afterward. A new term is a new class. A student who leaves closes your view of them at once, including what they shared before. Removing them does the same, and is final: they return only by entering the code and agreeing again.

Flip through, for practice together

Tap a deck or a lesson, then Flip through (or Flip through all, when some lessons are switched off). It shows each card Japanese side up, like a stack of paper flashcards, shuffled or in order. Nothing is graded, and nobody's schedule changes.

Without an account

A deck file you send is the student's own copy. It installs from Decks, Add a deck, Add from a file, without signing in, and studies offline exactly as with an account. A class run this way has nothing for you to read.

A progress file is a student's whole record, so it is theirs to offer, not yours to ask for. It carries more than joining a class shares. If a student chooses to hand you one (You, Settings, Export my progress), open You, Settings, For whoever teaches the course, Read student exports. Choose the course deck (one already on your device works), then load the progress files. You get the same four questions, and nothing leaves your device.

Privacy, plainly

A student's record is the reviews they did (which card, when, the grade they gave, how long it took), the cards they wrote themselves, which classes they joined, and the name they chose to show. Not their settings, not the course decks, never a password.

Only that student can read it, until they join a class. Then you read one part: their reviews of the decks you published to that class, once you approve them, and only until they leave, you remove them, or the class ends.

You never see a password or an e-mail address inside the app. Students sign in on the identity service's own page, and your roster shows the name each student typed for you. One tap leaves a class. You, then Delete account removes their record from the server; the copy on their own phone stays until they erase it in Settings.

On a phone's home screen

The simplest advice for students: use Kurikaeshi in the browser, signed in.

An installed copy (Share, then Add to Home Screen on an iPhone; Add to Home screen or Install app in Chrome on Android; Install in the address bar on a computer) opens full screen and offline, but it cannot sign in. Sign-in, adding course decks and classes happen in the browser; the installed copy keeps studying the cards it already holds.

Built from the course lists teachers supply and a licensed dictionary; every reading is checked against a named source rather than typed from memory. A list that arrives as a picture rather than text is read off the page by hand, and the deck says "not yet checked" on its source line until it has been compared to the original. Sources and licences are shown in the app under About Kurikaeshi.
